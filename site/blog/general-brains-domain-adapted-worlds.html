LOG 001 / WORLD MODELS / DOMAIN ADAPTATION / PHYSICAL AI

LLMs, world models,
and what comes next.

LLMs keep getting better. I think the next question is how their intelligence connects to the worlds where we use it.

Shamane Siriwardhana05 October 2026Updated 09 October 20267 min read

IN THIS LOG

LLMs and long-horizon agents have changed the landscape of digital work, from coding and research to longer workflows. So where does intelligence go next? I’m especially interested in how it extends into the physical world, and what still needs to be specific to a domain.

From self-supervised learning to world models

The progression I’m interested in starts with self-supervised learning. LLMs scaled that approach around language, and omni LLMs bring text, vision and audio together. Now the question is how that broader intelligence connects to different types of world models.

General models, longer work

Claude Code, Codex and computer-use agents have changed my expectations again. The work is getting longer. METR’s measurements show growing task horizons on software tasks, measured against the time a human expert needs. That is evidence of progress in those tasks, with reliability still part of the picture. [4]

There is also a possible feedback loop. More useful work produces more outcomes to evaluate. Good examples and failures can become training material. Better models can attempt harder work. The important step is deciding which feedback is actually useful. Usage alone does not make a model learn.

So I expect general capability to keep improving. But a model that can organize a project still needs grounded information about the environment where its decisions matter.

Worlds we can see

One direction is becoming very visible. Generate a scene, move through it, imagine what happens next. Fei-Fei Li’s spatial intelligence argument extends beyond pixels, and World Labs’ taxonomy separates the functions of rendering, simulation and planning while showing how they connect. [2] [3]

Cosmos 3 and Odyssey-3 show how broad this direction is becoming. Such systems can supply varied scenarios for training and evaluation. They can also learn policies and generate actions. Their role can extend from generating training scenarios to supplying deployed policies. [5] [6]

My feeling is that broad coverage and expensive training give large labs an advantage here. But size and deployment are separate choices. Cosmos 3 includes a 4B Edge variant. [5] And a world that looks convincing still needs to predict the consequences of actions well enough for its intended use.

Predicting what matters

JEPA-style models make another choice. They predict learned representations. That target need not reconstruct every leaf moving behind a robot. It can focus on what the representation preserves. The hard part is keeping small details that matter to the task, such as contact or geometry.

This can reduce the burden of prediction, but it does not guarantee fewer parameters, less data or fast on-device control. Generative models also use latent representations internally. The useful distinction is what the model is trained to predict and how that prediction is used.

V-JEPA 2-AC demonstrated action-conditioned prediction and robot planning. More recently, RoboJEPA scaled its predictor to 8B parameters and reported planning that takes seconds on multiple GPUs. It also decoded predicted latent states into video. That is a useful reminder that latent prediction, scale and rendering can coexist. [7] [8]

Yann LeCun’s AMI Labs is pursuing prediction in abstract representations of camera and other sensor data. I find that direction interesting because the useful state of a world does not have to look like a video. [9]

Different jobs, one system

This is how I currently connect the pieces. A language model helps set goals and coordinate tools. A generative or spatial world model provides environments, scenarios and possible futures. An action-conditioned predictor can help a planner compare the consequences of candidate actions using current observations. A policy or controller executes the chosen behavior.

These are useful roles, not fixed model families. One model may cover several. A robot might call a predictor during planning, or use a policy trained with world-model rollouts and avoid that call at runtime. RoboJEPA’s optional video decoding and Cosmos’ policy outputs already cross the boundaries.

The connection still needs work. Goals, observations and actions need compatible interfaces. Prediction errors grow over longer rollouts. And a useful deployment has to fit the latency and reliability requirements of the actual system.

Where domain adaptation goes next

This is the part I want to work on. A warehouse camera, a LiDAR point cloud, a satellite image and a machine’s sensor stream expose different information. Predicting their raw signals is possible, but it may be unnecessary for the decision we need to make.

JEPA-Anything explores a shared learning principle across seven domains using domain-specific encoders, adapters and prediction targets. It does not demonstrate one universal pretrained model for all of them. It also distinguishes learning useful representations from retaining a predictor for operational use. That distinction matters. A good embedding alone is not an action-conditioned world model. [10]

For a concrete sensor example, AD-LiST-JEPA explores LiDAR occupancy completion and forecasting. That is evidence for learning useful point-cloud predictions, not a complete autonomous controller. Geospatial change and industrial sensor forecasting are directions I would like to explore through the same question. What needs to be predicted for this particular decision? [11]

My bet is that reusable foundations and domain-specific prediction will grow together. V-JEPA 2-AC shows one route, reusing a frozen encoder while learning an action-conditioned predictor. Other sensors or tasks may need a different representation. [7] I care about making that adaptation accessible, so people can build and own useful intelligence for the worlds they actually work in.
