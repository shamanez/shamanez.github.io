LOG 001 / WORLD MODELS / DOMAIN ADAPTATION / PHYSICAL AI

General brains,
domain-adapted worlds.

One shared brain, one adapted world model per site. My bet on the next chapter of domain adaptation, and how learned world models can improve simulations, digital twins and physical AI.

Shamane Siriwardhana05 October 202610 min read

Intelligence feels increasingly general now, right? But physical AI is a different story. So this is my bet: domain adaptation is not dead. A major part of its next chapter will be in world models that understand the particular places where machines act.

From self-supervised foundation models to world models

Self-supervised learning gave us a powerful recipe: pretrain on large amounts of unlabeled text, vision or speech, then reuse the representations or adapt the model. Domain adaptation was part of my own work on RAG: helping a general system work with knowledge from a particular domain. [1]

Then autoregressive language models scaled up: GPT-2, GPT-3, Llama, GPT-4. The big thing happened when these transformers became useful across more and more tasks without a separate model for each one. [2] [3]

World models were developing alongside them. Ha and Schmidhuber showed how a learned, compressed model of an environment could support an agent trained in imagination. Dreamer 3 later worked across more than 150 tasks with one algorithm configuration. [4] [5]

Now that work is much more visible. Genie 3 generates interactive environments in real time. Dreamer 4 trains behaviors inside a learned world model, drawing much of its knowledge from unlabeled video. Its demonstration is in Minecraft. World Labs presents Atlas as a model for generation, reconstruction and space-time simulation. The generalization arc that language went through is starting to appear in physical and spatial AI too. [6] [7] [8]

Intelligence is going general

Claude Code, Codex and computer-use agents have changed what I expect from a general model. They can handle longer sequences of work, and that is a big shift. METR's 2025 study found that the task horizon reached at 50% success had historically doubled roughly every seven months on its software and reasoning tasks, with task length measured by human completion time. That is a benchmark trend, not a promise that every job is automated. [9]

For many projects, my default is increasingly to use a strong shared model rather than host a language model just to save on tokens. Open-weight and smaller models still matter when privacy, cost at scale, offline operation or customization make them the right choice. But competing on general language intelligence alone is a hard place to build a lasting advantage.

So where does domain-specific work live next? For me, the interesting question is moving beyond another fine-tuning service or another agent wrapper. What does the general brain still need when it leaves the screen?

Then comes the physical world

It is clear to me that physical AI is a huge next chapter. Warehouses, farms, factories and hospitals are full of work that cannot be finished with a browser click.

With robots come simulators, digital twins and all the tools for describing and testing a physical system. A strong language model can help reason about goals and coordinate those tools. Small language models can play useful roles too. But language capability alone does not tell a robot how an unfamiliar surface, payload or contact will behave. [10]

What the big brain needs is a reliable way to ask: What happens here if this machine takes this action? That is where I see learned world models adding something specific.

World models, simulators and digital twins

Here, by learned world model, I mean a model trained to predict how an environment changes, often conditioned on actions. That prediction can be a video, a structured state or a compact latent representation. It is useful because an agent can compare possible futures before choosing what to do. [15]

Generative models such as Genie 3, Atlas and Cosmos emphasize rich visual or spatial predictions. Latent approaches such as Dreamer, TD-MPC2 and V-JEPA 2-AC let planning or policy learning happen in a learned representation rather than requiring a rendered movie for every candidate future. These are overlapping approaches, and both can be made specific to an application. [5] [6] [8] [11] [12] [19]

A simulator and a digital twin sit at a different level. A simulator runs an environment using engineered dynamics, learned dynamics or a mixture. A digital twin represents a particular real system and stays connected to information about that system. Neither term, by itself, specifies a learned world-model architecture. A world model can be a component that improves either workflow. [13]

How learned world models can improve existing tools

SimulatorITS ROLERun an environment to train, test or compare behavior.WHAT A WORLD MODEL CAN ADDLearn dynamics corrections or generate richer, more varied observations.
Digital twinITS ROLERepresent a particular physical system using its models and real observations.WHAT A WORLD MODEL CAN ADDImprove local predictions alongside the twin’s engineering models and data.

Take a warehouse twin with its geometry, conveyor layout and robot kinematics. A learned world model could improve predictions about how boxes move, or learn residual corrections where simulated dynamics disagree with real observations. ReDRAW demonstrates that kind of correction in latent dynamics. For a different contribution, Cosmos can turn controlled Omniverse 3D scenarios into richer visual training data. Better dynamics and more varied observations can both improve simulation and twin workflows. They address different gaps. [14] [21]

That is the relationship I mean: world models augment simulations and digital twins. The existing geometry, engineering constraints and connection to the real system still matter. A plausible generated future is not automatically a physically accurate one.

So this is my concept

One shared brain, one adapted world model per site.

A warehouse floor, a surgical robot and a strawberry farm share some physics, but differ in their objects, contacts, sensors and operating conditions. Useful knowledge should transfer. The remaining mismatch is where local data and adaptation can matter.

There are several kinds of specificity here. A domain might be warehouse manipulation. A task might be picking a box. A site adds the actual camera setup, floor, payloads and changing conditions. Adapting the world model means improving its predictions for those conditions. Adapting the policy means improving how the robot acts. They can support each other, but they are different jobs.

The general reasoning layer can stay shared. A local world model supplies predictions relevant to its site, and the robot's controller executes the actions. The cloud brain can set goals and ask for lookahead without becoming the machine's entire control loop.

I see three useful roles for that local model:

  1. Lookahead. Predict the outcomes of candidate actions before execution. WebDreamer explores a similar idea for web agents. Applying it to robots requires grounding the predictions in physical observations and actions. [16]
  2. Training and evaluation. Give policies an imagined environment in which to learn or be compared before real trials. Dreamer demonstrates imagination training, and GigaWorld-1 focuses on world models for robot policy evaluation. Neither removes the need to validate on the real machine. [5] [18]
  3. Progress estimation. With a goal representation or a learned reward/value model, help estimate whether a predicted state moves the task forward. A predictor does not automatically know what “good” means. That objective has to enter the system. [12] [20]

There are already concrete routes to adaptation. Cosmos provides pretrained models and post-training examples. V-JEPA 2 starts with action-free video pretraining. V-JEPA 2-AC then learns an action-conditioned predictor on a frozen encoder using under 62 hours of DROID robot interaction video. It planned pick-and-place actions in two new labs without target-site training. That is encouraging evidence for transfer, not evidence that every site must start from scratch. [12] [19]

Another route starts with simulation. SimDist transfers its encoder, reward model and value function, then updates the latent dynamics from real observations. ReDRAW learns residual corrections to a simulation-pretrained world model with limited target data. These are concrete examples of adapting physical predictions. My bet is that this becomes a repeatable deployment practice as sites change. [20] [21]

“One per site” is the architecture I am betting on. It could mean a shared base with a small local adapter, a calibrated dynamics module or a locally updated model. The important thing is that the system knows where its general predictions stop being accurate.

Why this is the place I want to be

Three reasons.

First, the data. The internet does not tell you exactly how your conveyor belt behaves today. Local video, telemetry and action outcomes expose the errors that matter at that site. The advantage is turning that experience into better predictions and keeping them current.

Second, latency. Hosting a language model only to shave token costs is less interesting to me than putting a useful predictive model near the robot. Local inference can avoid a network round trip where timing matters. But “300M” is not a universal size target: V-JEPA 2-AC's approximately 300M predictor still uses a larger encoder. The whole perception, planning and control pipeline has to meet the application's timing budget. [12]

Third, the adaptation surface. We do not necessarily need to retrain everything. A frozen encoder with an adapted predictor, dynamics-only updates in SimDist, or residual corrections in ReDRAW can reuse general knowledge while changing the part that is wrong locally. That is the kind of domain adaptation I want to build. [12] [20] [21]

What still has to work

I am not pretending this is solved. Prediction errors compound over long rollouts. Contact, deformable objects and unfamiliar conditions remain hard. GigaWorld-1 finds that action-faithful, consistent rollouts matter more for policy evaluation than pretty short clips. RepWAM's results also suggest that representations useful for action can matter more than optimizing pixel reconstruction alone. [18] [22]

The interface between the big brain and the world model is still an engineering question. I want a tool that takes the current observations, candidate actions and a goal, then returns useful consequences, scores and uncertainty where that uncertainty is calibrated. WorldEvolver explores memory revision and filtering low-confidence foresight in text-based agent environments. That is a useful design clue, not a finished robotics interface. [17]

But none of that changes where I see the work. General models will keep improving. The world under each robot will keep changing. Someone has to close that gap: improve the predictions, strengthen the simulations and digital twins they support, and make those predictions useful to the agent.

General brains, domain-adapted worlds. That is my bet.
